The idea in one paragraph

A gamma line at a given energy could come from many places: a decay in the uranium or thorium chain, a cosmogenic isotope, a neutron capture, an alpha-induced reaction, or a detector effect such as an escape peak. The explorer keeps a database of all of these, estimates how many gammas per second each would produce in your setup (detector, location, materials and their masses), and uses that expected rate as a prior. Every tool on the page then combines two things: how well a source matches what you see, and how plausible it is that the source is there at all.

Ranking candidate sources

For each source the explorer computes an expected emission rate R (gammas per second) from a physical model where one exists:

decays: R = Σ_materials mass × activity of the chain segment (Bq/kg) × branching × line intensity neutron capture: R = Σ_materials mass × Φ_thermal × (target atoms per kg) × σ_thermal × gammas per capture inelastic (n,n′γ): R = Σ_materials mass × Φ_fast × (target atoms per kg) × σ (assumed 1 b, shared over levels) alpha-induced: R = Σ_materials mass × Σ_segments alpha activity × thick-target gamma yield per alpha

Activities are medians of radiopurity.org assay records; compositions, fluxes and cross sections are cited on the explorer page and in each source's “Sources” list. Sources without a rate model (cosmogenic, man-made, radon in air, X-ray fluorescence) get a coarse prior on a fixed reference scale. Isotopes with no reason to be near your detector are ranked below all neutron- and alpha-induced lines.

Rates span many orders of magnitude, so the ranking compresses them: a line's relevance is 1 for the strongest candidate and falls by 1/8 per factor of ten below it, reaching 0 eight decades down. Under Adjust the ranking you can multiply whole categories, or single sources, by factors of ten. That changes the ranking and the priors used by the fit, but not the physical rates shown.

Identifying a list of peaks

Given peak energies, each candidate line gets a match score exp(−½ (ΔE/σ)²), where σ is the detector resolution at that energy (widened for Doppler-broadened lines). Decay-chain segments in secular equilibrium, such as ²²²Rn with ²¹⁴Pb and ²¹⁴Bi, are treated as one hypothesis, because their lines must appear together with fixed ratios. Hypotheses are then chosen greedily: the one that explains the most peaks, weighted by its expected rate, is accepted first, and its other strong lines are checked. A source whose strong lines are missing loses credit, and candidates more than about six decades weaker than the strongest are kept only as alternatives.

Fitting a whole spectrum

The Fit a spectrum tab goes further: it fits every channel of a measured spectrum at once. The model for the expected counts μi in channel i is

μ_i = Σ_j β_j B_j(E_i) + Σ_k A_k T_k(E_i; θ) T_k(E; θ) = Σ_lines I_line · ε(E_line) · R(E; E_line, θ)
  • Bj are smooth cubic B-splines with non-negative weights βj: the background. It absorbs anything smooth that the templates don't describe, such as gammas scattered in shielding or the sample.
  • Tk is the template of candidate source k: all of its lines with their database intensities I and the relative efficiency ε, each turned into a detector response R. One amplitude Ak per source means its lines are fitted together, with ratios fixed by nuclear data.
  • θ are the response parameters: calibration, resolution, efficiency shape and peak or Compton shape. Each has a value and an uncertainty that you set, i.e. a Gaussian prior.

The response function

energy calibration: E = c₀ + c₁·channel + c₂·channel² resolution: FWHM(E)² = a² + b·E + c·E² (electronic noise, counting statistics, other) relative efficiency: ln ε = e₁·x + e₂·x², x = ln(E / 1 MeV)

The resolution form covers both germanium and scintillators: for HPGe the noise term a and the statistical (Fano) term b dominate; for scintillators the b·E term, i.e. FWHM ∝ √E, does. The efficiency is relative, because the overall scale goes into each source's amplitude; only its energy dependence matters for line ratios.

Two response models are available:

  • Peaks + smooth background (HPGe). Each line is a Gaussian photopeak plus a small low-energy step, from incomplete charge collection and small-angle scattering, with height set as a fraction of the peak height (as in the Hypermet peak model of Phillips & Marlow). An optional exponential low-energy tail is available too. Above 1022 keV, single- and double-escape peaks are added, growing linearly with energy and reaching the escape parameters at 2614 keV. The Compton continuum is left to the smooth background, which is what standard HPGe peak fitting does and works well at germanium resolution.
  • Peaks + Compton continuum (scintillators, liquid nobles). At poor resolution the continuum carries much of the information, so each line gets the full shape described next.

The Compton continuum

A gamma of energy E that Compton-scatters once and escapes leaves an electron with kinetic energy T between 0 and the Compton edge,

T_max = E · 2ε / (1 + 2ε), ε = E / mₑc²

distributed according to the Klein–Nishina cross section (Klein & Nishina 1929; see Knoll, ch. 10):

dσ/dT ∝ 2 + s² / [ε² (1 − s)²] + [s / (1 − s)] · (s − 2/ε), s = T / E

A real detector adds three things, each controlled by a parameter:

  • Peak-to-total ratio P/T(E): the share of interacting gammas that deposit their full energy, modelled as P/T(662 keV) × (E / 662 keV)−k. Larger detectors have higher P/T.
  • Multiple-scatter shelf: gammas that scatter more than once can deposit energies between the edge and the photopeak; a flat shelf carries this share of the non-photopeak counts.
  • Backscatter peak: gammas scattered by about 180° in nearby material and then absorbed, at E / (1 + 2ε), roughly 200–250 keV; a broad Gaussian carries this share.

The single-scatter continuum takes the remaining share. The continuum is built per channel, normalized over its whole range from 0 to Tmax (not just the fitted channels), and smeared with the energy-dependent resolution; the photopeak is smeared analytically.

This semi-empirical shape needs no simulation and works reasonably for moderate-size scintillators once P/T, shelf and backscatter are calibrated, for example with ¹³⁷Cs, ⁶⁰Co and ²²⁸Th spectra. For the best accuracy, a simulated response matrix of the actual detector and shielding (for example from Geant4) would replace it; that is planned as an option.

The algorithm

The fit maximizes the Poisson likelihood of the counts, equivalently minimizes the deviance (the likelihood-ratio χ² of Baker & Cousins), plus the prior penalty Σ((θ − θ₀)/σ)² for the response parameters. Three facts make it fast enough for a browser:

  1. Linear in the amplitudes. For fixed θ, μ is linear in the background weights β and source amplitudes A, all of which must be non-negative. The multiplicative expectation-maximization update of Shepp & Vardi (MLEM),
    x_k ← x_k · Σ_i T_k(i) n_i / μ_i / Σ_i T_k(i)
    keeps every amplitude non-negative and increases the likelihood at every step. A little over-relaxation speeds it up, and templates are stored sparsely, so each step costs only the channels a source actually touches.
  2. Few nonlinear parameters. θ (at most about 15) is fitted by Levenberg–Marquardt on the signed deviance residuals plus prior pulls, re-solving the amplitudes after each accepted step (variable projection, Golub & Pereyra). Derivatives are taken with the amplitudes held fixed (Kaufman's simplification), which keeps them cheap and noise-free.
  3. Source selection by backward elimination. The fit starts with the top candidates from the ranking (60 by default). A source is kept only if removing it worsens the deviance by more than a threshold: Δχ² ≥ 9 by default, about 3σ, plus 2·ln(1/prior) for sources the ranking considers unlikely. So an implausible isotope needs much stronger evidence than ⁴⁰K. Sources the fit gives nothing to are dropped together, then the weakest remaining one at a time; each removal test refits locally, only the channels that source affects. Selection runs once with the starting response and again after θ is fitted.

Uncertainties come from the Fisher information of the final fit over all active amplitudes and response parameters together, including the prior, so a source's uncertainty includes its correlation with, say, the resolution or the peak-to-total ratio. The fit runs in a background thread (a Web Worker); a typical HPGe spectrum takes a few seconds.

Reading the results

  • Counts in range: counts from that source inside the fit range: its photopeaks in HPGe mode, photopeaks plus continuum in Compton mode. Divide by live time and efficiency to get activities.
  • Significance: √Δχ² when the source is removed and the rest refitted locally: a measure of how much the data need it.
  • Pulls of the response parameters: (fitted − your value)/your uncertainty. Beyond ±3 the data disagree with what you told it; check the calibration or detector settings.
  • Goodness of fit: deviance per degree of freedom near 1 is good. Much larger means something is missing; the residual panel under the plot shows where.

Limits to keep in mind

Calibration must start close. The fit refines the calibration but cannot find it from scratch: start within a few FWHM, for example by identifying two strong peaks first.

Compton mode carries model systematics. The smooth background and the continuum shape partly trade off. In tests on simulated NaI spectra the amplitudes came out within about 5–10% of the truth, a few times the quoted statistical uncertainty. Calibrating P/T and the shelf with known sources reduces this.

Not yet modelled: true-coincidence summing (cascades such as ⁶⁰Co and ²¹⁴Bi in close geometry), pile-up and dead time, X-ray escape peaks, and a full efficiency curve below about 100 keV. Overlapping lines closer than the resolution cannot be separated; the priors then decide which source gets the credit.

Candidates come from the database and your setup. A source that is not in the database, or is ranked below the candidate cut, cannot be found: raise “Candidate sources” or adjust the ranking if you suspect one.

The examples on the fit tab are simulated with this same model, so they show how the tool behaves, not how well the model describes a real detector.

Decay chains

The Decay chains tab follows each chain from its parent through the ENSDF decay modes, keeping branches of at least 10⁻⁵ %. For every member it lists the half-life, spin-parity, decay modes with branching ratios and Q-values, alpha and beta spectra, every gamma line with its initial and final level, multipolarity and internal-conversion coefficient, and the X-rays.

Relative activity is the member's activity in secular equilibrium, relative to the parent: 1 for the main line, the product of branching ratios on side branches (for example 0.359 for ²⁰⁸Tl). Real samples can be out of equilibrium, through radon escape, chemical separation of radium or lead, or ²¹⁰Pb accumulation; the full time dependence follows Bateman's equations.

Emitted together with gives, for each gamma, the probability that another gamma of the same decay accompanies it. It is computed by cascading through the daughter's levels: the populations of the levels come from the gamma intensities, and from each level the branching follows the gammas leaving it. These pairs are what coincidence measurements see, and what produces sum peaks when the source is close to the detector.
